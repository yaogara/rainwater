The Complete Rainwater Collection System for Homes: Tanks, Filters & Diverters Explained

By Rainwater Directory Team

•

Updated November 22, 2026

A well-engineered residential rainwater harvesting system is far more than a plastic barrel tucked under a downspout. Whether you are designing a 200-gallon drip-irrigation setup or a 10,000-gallon whole-home supply, every dependable system relies on six coordinated subsystems working together in sequence.

Skipping or undersizing a single upstream step—such as gutter leaf screening or first-flush diversion—cascades downward into clogged pumps, anaerobic tank odor, and prematurely fouled filters.

How Much Rain Can Your Roof Actually Collect?

According to Texas A&M AgriLife Extension and the University of Arizona Cooperative Extension (az1344), potential annual rainwater collection follows a straightforward hydrologic formula:

Annual Gallons = Roof Footprint (sq ft) × Annual Rainfall (in) × 0.6233 × Collection Efficiency

  • 0.6233 converts 1 inch of rain over 1 square foot into U.S. gallons (144 cu in / 231 cu in per gal).
  • Collection efficiency (typically 0.75–0.90) accounts for splash-over, evaporation off warm shingles, and water rejected by leaf screens and first-flush diverters. Note that roof footprint is the horizontal projected area covered by the roof—not the slanted shingle surface area.

Screening vs. Storage Sizing

Two separate questions govern system design:

  1. Supply potential: How many gallons can your roof capture over a year or rainy season?
  2. Demand & reserve target: How many gallons per day do your landscape, livestock, toilets, or household require during the longest expected dry spell?

1. Catchment Surface and Roof Hygiene

Metal roofs (standing-seam painted steel or factory-coated galvalume) and glazed tile shed water quickly with minimal grit, offering the highest collection efficiency (~0.85–0.90) and cleanest runoff. Composition asphalt shingles shed mineral granules that require heavier pre-screening and are generally discouraged for potable systems unless certified free of harmful leachates. Keep tree branches trimmed back from eaves to minimize leaf litter and animal droppings.

2. Gutters, Downspouts, and Wet vs. Dry Conveyance

Size gutters and downspouts for peak cloudburst intensity—not just annual rainfall totals. Texas A&M AgriLife Extension and Virginia Tech Extension (BSE-265) cite roughly 1 square inch of downspout cross-section per 100 square feet of roof catchment.

  • Dry conveyance: Overhead piping slopes continuously above ground into the tank inlet so pipes drain completely empty between storms—preferred wherever freezing occurs.
  • Wet conveyance (“charged” underground lines): Downspouts drop underground and rise back up into an elevated tank inlet. Wet lines allow collection from multiple sides of a house into one tank, but retain standing water in buried horizontal runs and require frost-depth burial and low-point flush drains.

3. Leaf Screens and First-Flush Diverters

Never pipe raw gutters straight into a sealed cistern.

  • Coarse screening: Angled stainless-steel wedge-wire or vortex screens (such as 400–1,000 micron self-cleaning hoods) reject leaves and twigs before water enters storage.
  • First-flush diversion: Diverts the initial dirty wash carrying roof pollen, dust, and droppings into a closed standpipe chamber before clean water overflows into the cistern. See our detailed Downspout & First-Flush Diverter Guide for exact pipe-profile dimensions and university extension volume rules.

4. Storage Cisterns and Passive Tank Protection

Whatever vessel you select—a slimline poly tank beside a garage, a modular corrugated steel cistern with a food-grade liner, or a buried concrete vault—every engineered cistern needs:

  • 100% light opacity to stop algae growth.
  • Calming inlet diffuser near the tank bottom to minimize turbulence.
  • Screened overflow sized equal to or larger than the combined inlet pipe area, routed safely away from building foundations.
  • Lockable manway access and 1/16” mosquito-proof stainless mesh on all vents and overflows.

Compare materials and standards (ASTM D1998, AWWA D100, ACI 350) in our 10 Water Storage Tank Types Guide or explore buried designs in Cistern Systems 101.

5. Pumps, Pressure Tanks, and Municipal Makeup Water

Gravity systems yield roughly 0.433 PSI per foot of elevation between the water surface and the outlet—fine for low-pressure drip irrigation if elevated a few feet, but far short of household sprinkler or indoor pressures (40–60 PSI). Whole-property systems use a submersible or external variable-speed booster pump paired with a captive-air pressure tank and low-water float cutoff.

If your cistern includes an automatic municipal water top-up valve for droughts, plumbing codes require a true air gap (discharge above the flood rim of the tank) or a tested ASSE 5110 Reduced Pressure Zone (RPZ) backflow preventer so stored rainwater can never enter city mains.

6. End-Use Treatment: Irrigation vs. Whole-House Potable

Match water treatment strictly to intended use:

  • Landscape & drip irrigation: Course leaf screen + first-flush + 100–200 mesh inline Y-strainer to keep drip emitters clear.
  • Indoor non-potable (toilet flushing / laundry): Graded sediment (20 → 5 µm) + carbon block for odor/color + UV or chlorination if required by local plumbing code, plus purple pipe labeling.
  • Potable drinking water: Full multi-barrier train (5 µm sediment → NSF/ANSI 53 carbon → NSF/ANSI 55 Class A UV ≥ 40 mJ/cm²), regular coliform testing, and health department compliance. Read our CDC Potable Rainwater Guide and Whole-House Filtration Guide.
