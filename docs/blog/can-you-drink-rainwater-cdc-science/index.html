Can You Drink Rainwater? Here’s What Science and the CDC Say

By Rainwater Directory Team

•

Updated November 22, 2026

No—untreated rainwater is not considered safe to drink. While rain starts as condensed atmospheric vapor, it picks up airborne particulates as it falls and washes animal waste, organic debris, and roofing chemicals off your catchment surface before entering storage.

According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), roof-collected rainwater systems have been linked to disease outbreaks when consumed untreated or poorly maintained. Where local plumbing and health codes allow potable rainwater harvesting, making roof runoff safe to drink requires inert catchment materials, first-flush diversion, multi-stage filtration, validated disinfection, routine water testing, and backflow separation.

Why Untreated Rainwater Is Not Potable

Public health agencies classify untreated roof runoff as non-potable by default because contamination enters at three distinct points:

  1. Atmospheric washout: Falling rain scavenges dust, pollen, wildfire smoke, soot, and dissolved trace gases.
  2. Catchment surface accumulation: Between storms, roofs collect bird and rodent droppings (Salmonella, Campylobacter, Cryptosporidium, Giardia), windblown soil, leaves, insects, and moss.
  3. Materials and storage degradation: Certain roof coatings, treated wood shakes, copper/zinc flashings, older lead solder, or non-food-grade tanks and hoses can leach metals or synthetic organics into slightly acidic rainwater.

Catchment Materials: Start With a Potable-Safe Roof

Treatment works best when you minimize chemical leaching before water ever enters the tank:

  • Preferred surfaces: Standing-seam painted steel, factory-baked enamel metal, unpainted aluminum, glazed clay or concrete tile, or slate paired with lead-free gutters and sealants. Look for NSF/ANSI Protocol P151 certification on specialized rainwater catchment coatings and roofing products.
  • Surfaces to avoid for drinking water: Asphalt/fiberglass composite shingles (can shed grit and hydrocarbon binders), wood shakes treated with copper or creosote preservatives, exposed copper roofs/gutters (copper leaching into low-pH rainwater), and roofs with lead flashing or lead-based paint.
  • Overhanging vegetation: Trim tree limbs away from the roof to reduce leaf tannins, bird roosting, and rodent access.

The Multi-Barrier Potable Treatment Train

University of Arizona Cooperative Extension (Preparing Rainwater for Potable Use, az1863) and ARCSA/ASPE/ANSI Standard 63 emphasize layered barriers rather than relying on any single device. Each upstream stage protects the stages downstream:

  1. Coarse leaf screening: Corrosion-resistant gutter guards and angled basket screens keep leaves and insects out of downspouts before organics decay in the water column.
  2. First-flush diversion: A standpipe or floating-ball diverter routes the dirtiest initial roof runoff away from the cistern after dry periods. Drain and clean the diverter chamber after storms so stagnant water does not breed mosquitoes or microbes.
  3. Opaque, food-grade storage: Cisterns must block sunlight completely (preventing algae growth) and comply with NSF/ANSI/CAN 61 (drinking water system component health effects) and NSF/ANSI/CAN 372 (lead-free). Fit inlets with calming diffusers so incoming water does not stir up bottom sediment, and equip overflows with backflow/vermin screens.
  4. Graded sediment filtration: Step down from a coarse 20–50 micron prefilter to a 5-micron or finer sediment cartridge. Reducing turbidity is mandatory—suspended particles physically shield pathogens from UV light.
  5. Activated carbon filtration: An NSF/ANSI 53 or NSF/ANSI 42 activated carbon block reduces tastes, odors, color, and trace organic compounds prior to disinfection.
  6. Microbiological disinfection (NSF/ANSI 55 Class A UV):
    • NSF/ANSI 55 Class A UV systems deliver a minimum dose of 40 mJ/cm² (40,000 µW·s/cm²) designed to inactivate bacteria, viruses, Giardia, and Cryptosporidium in visually clear water (<1 NTU turbidity). Class B UV units are not validated as primary pathogen barriers on untreated water.
    • Keep the quartz sleeve clean and replace the UV lamp on the manufacturer’s schedule (typically every 12 months), because UV output degrades even while the bulb still glows blue.
    • For larger systems or commercial/public buildings, jurisdictions often also require metered chlorine residual or point-of-entry monitoring.

Plumbing Code, Backflow Protection, and Legality

Before designing a drinking-water catchment system, check both water-rights law and local health/plumbing departments:

  • State rules vary sharply: Some states explicitly allow residential potable rainwater systems with treatment and permitting, while others restrict collection volume or limit captured rainwater to outdoor irrigation. Review our State-by-State Rainwater Legality Guide.
  • Zero cross-connections: Never tie a rainwater line into municipal tap plumbing without approved physical separation (such as an air gap) or a tested Reduced Pressure Zone (RPZ) backflow assembly inspected by an ASSE 5110 certified tester. Backflow incidents can contaminate neighborhood drinking mains.
  • Pipe labeling: Plumbing codes such as the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) require purple or clearly labeled piping on non-potable rainwater distribution lines to prevent accidental consumption.

Routine Testing and Maintenance Schedule

Unlike municipal tap water, private rainwater systems place testing and upkeep on the property owner:

  • After every major storm / monthly: Inspect gutter screens, flush and reset first-flush diverters, and verify tank lids, gaskets, and overflow screens remain sealed.
  • Every 3–6 months: Inspect and replace sediment and activated carbon cartridges based on pressure drop or manufacturer gallon rating.
  • Annually: Replace the UV-C lamp, clean or replace the quartz sleeve, test total coliform and E. coli through a state-certified drinking-water lab, and inspect cistern sediment depth.
  • After wildfire smoke, roof repairs, or floodwater exposure: Divert the roof away from storage until washed clean. If floodwaters ever enter a buried or low-lying cistern, treat the tank as contaminated and follow CDC cistern cleaning and shock-chlorination disinfection procedures before any use.
